1Theory on one page

Exam-ready answers for every definition question asked so far. The orange tag shows where each one was asked.

Game 2022
A competitive situation is called a game if (i) there is a finite number of competitors, called players; (ii) each player has a finite number of strategies (courses of action); (iii) a play takes place when each player uses one of their strategies; (iv) every play ends in an outcome (gain, loss or draw) called the payoff.
Two-person zero-sum game 2024 · S2026
A game with exactly two players in which the gain of one player equals the loss of the other, so the sum of the payoffs is always zero. The play only redistributes money between them. It is also called a rectangular game, because it can be written as one $m \times n$ matrix.
Payoff matrix S2026
If A has strategies $A_1, \dots, A_m$ and B has $B_1, \dots, B_n$, the payoff matrix is the $m \times n$ matrix $(a_{ij})$, where $a_{ij}$ is A's gain when A plays $A_i$ and B plays $B_j$. B's payoff matrix is $(-a_{ij})$ (written with B's strategies as rows, it is the negative transpose). Adding the two always gives the zero matrix.

Example (class notes): both players toss a coin. B pays A Rs 7 for (H, H) and Rs 4 for (T, T); otherwise A pays B Rs 3.

Strategy; pure and mixed strategy 2024 · S2026
A strategy is the rule, decided in advance, by which a player chooses a course of action.
  • Pure strategy: the player always picks one particular course of action (probability 1). Example: always play "Heads".
  • Mixed strategy: the player picks each course of action with a fixed probability: $p = (p_1, \dots, p_m)$, with $p_i \ge 0$ and $\sum p_i = 1$. Example: play Heads with probability ½ and Tails with ½. The objective is then to maximise the expected gain (or minimise the expected loss).
A pure strategy is the special mixed strategy with one $p_i = 1$.
Optimal strategy; value of the game 2024 · S2026
An optimal strategy gives a player the best expected payoff they can guarantee, whatever the opponent does. The value $v$ of the game is the expected payoff (to A) when both players use optimal strategies. With mixed strategies $p$ and $q$ the expected payoff is $E(p, q) = \sum_i \sum_j p_i\, a_{ij}\, q_j$.
Maximin–minimax principle; minimax criterion 2022 · 2024 · S2026
Player A (maximiser) assumes the worst: in each row, the smallest entry is all that row is guaranteed to give. A then picks the row whose minimum is largest: maximin $= \max_i \min_j a_{ij}$ (the lower value of the game). Player B (minimiser) looks at the largest entry of each column (the most B can lose) and picks the column whose maximum is smallest: minimax $= \min_j \max_i a_{ij}$ (the upper value). Always $$\text{maximin} \;\le\; v \;\le\; \text{minimax}.$$ Minimax criterion of optimality: each player chooses the strategy that optimises their worst case. A maximises their minimum gain, B minimises their maximum loss. With mixed strategies, A chooses $p$ to maximise $\min_j E(p, B_j)$ and B chooses $q$ to minimise $\max_i E(A_i, q)$. Minimax theorem (von Neumann): with mixed strategies allowed, the two are always equal, so every finite two-person zero-sum game has a value.

Example (class notes): row minima are 1, −4, −1, so maximin = 1. Column maxima are 1, 5, 1, so minimax = 1. They are equal, so A plays $A_1$ and B plays $B_1$ or $B_3$, with value 1.

Saddle point (equilibrium point) 2024 · S2026
An entry $a_{rs}$ that is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column. It exists exactly when maximin = minimax. The players then use the pure strategies $A_r$ and $B_s$, and $v = a_{rs}$. Neither player gains by changing strategy alone, which is why it is also called an equilibrium point.
Strictly determinable game; fair game 2022 · 2024 · S2026
Strictly determinable: maximin = minimax (= $v$), i.e. the game has a saddle point and is solved in pure strategies. Fair: the value is $v = 0$, so neither player has an advantage. The two ideas are independent: $\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\-1&4\end{bmatrix}$ has a saddle point with value 0, so it is both. A game with a saddle value of 1 is strictly determinable but not fair. A mixed game with value $\tfrac{49}{11}$ is neither.
Assumptions of game theory 2024
(1) Each player has a finite number of strategies (the lists need not be the same). (2) Players act rationally and intelligently. (3) The list of strategies of each player, and the payoff for every choice, are known to both players in advance. (4) One player tries to maximise gains and the other to minimise losses. (5) Both players decide individually, without communicating. (6) Both choose and announce their strategies simultaneously, so neither benefits from knowing the other's choice. (7) The payoff is fixed and determined in advance.
Relationship between game theory and LP 2022 · 2024
Every finite two-person zero-sum game can be written as a pair of linear programs that are duals of each other. With $v > 0$ (add a constant if needed) and $x_i = p_i/v$, $y_j = q_j/v$: $$\text{A: Min } \textstyle\sum x_i \text{ s.t. } \sum_i a_{ij}x_i \ge 1\ (\forall j),\quad\text{B: Max } \sum y_j \text{ s.t. } \sum_j a_{ij}y_j \le 1\ (\forall i),\qquad v = \frac{1}{\text{optimal value}}.$$ Solving one LP by simplex gives both players' strategies: B's from the solution, A's from the dual values. The duality theorem (both optima are equal) is exactly the minimax theorem. This is how games with no saddle point that cannot be reduced to 2×n or m×2 are solved. Details in §6.

Useful facts

  • Check theorem (class notes): if $E(p^*, B_j) \ge v$ for every column $j$ and $E(A_i, q^*) \le v$ for every row $i$, then $p^*, q^*$ are optimal and $v$ is the value. Use it to check every answer (§7).
  • Adding a constant $c$ to every entry adds $c$ to the value and leaves the optimal strategies unchanged. Multiplying every entry by $k > 0$ multiplies the value by $k$.
  • A 2×2 game has a saddle point if and only if one row or column dominates the other.

Always the first thing you do. It takes thirty seconds and either finishes the question or tells you the value lies in an interval.

Case 2.5

Trap

Getting min and max the wrong way round

The saddle entry is the minimum of its row and the maximum of its column. Not the max of the row. A saddle point only ever needs those two checks; the rest of the matrix doesn't matter.

Parameter questions: "find the range of p, q"

Rule

Entry $a_{rs}$ is a saddle point ⇔ $a_{rs} \le$ every other entry of row r and $a_{rs} \ge$ every other entry of column s. Write these inequalities only for the unknowns that sit in row r or column s.

Case 2.6

Unknowns in the saddle's row or column

2022 Q3a(b): $\begin{bmatrix}2&4&5\\10&\mathbf{7}&q\\4&p&6\end{bmatrix}$, make (2,2) a saddle point.

$q$ is in row 2, so it must be ≥ 7 (7 must stay the row minimum). $p$ is in column 2, so it must be ≤ 7 (7 must stay the column maximum). Answer: $p \le 7,\ q \ge 7$, with $v = 7$.

Case 2.7

Unknown outside that row and column

$\begin{bmatrix}2&4&5\\10&\mathbf{7}&8\\r&6&6\end{bmatrix}$, make (2,2) a saddle point.

$r$ is in row 3 and column 1, so it never enters the check. Row 2: 7 ≤ 10, 8 ✓. Column 2: 7 ≥ 4, 6 ✓. So (2,2) is a saddle point for every value of $r$. Say this explicitly.

Case 2.8

Unknown in several cells, no position given

S2026: "for what λ is the game strictly determinable?" with λ on the diagonal.

Write each row minimum and column maximum as an expression in λ, e.g. $\min(\lambda, 2)$. Then maximin $= \min(\lambda, 2)$ and minimax $= \max(\lambda, -1)$. Solve maximin = minimax by splitting into ranges of λ. This gives $-1 \le \lambda \le 2$. Full working in S2026 Q3b.

Case 2.9

The unknown is the saddle entry itself

$\begin{bmatrix}3&5&8\\9&\boldsymbol{\lambda}&7\\5&4&7\end{bmatrix}$, make (2,2) a saddle point.

λ must be ≤ the rest of its row ($9, 7$ ⇒ $\lambda \le 7$) and ≥ the rest of its column ($5, 4$ ⇒ $\lambda \ge 5$). So $5 \le \lambda \le 7$, and the value is $v = \lambda$. In general you get the interval [largest other entry of the column, smallest other entry of the row]. If that interval is empty, the cell can never be a saddle point.

Case 2.10

≤ or <?

With equality, e.g. $q = 7$ above, the entry is still a saddle point; another saddle point with the same value simply appears beside it (Case 2.2). So write $p \le 7,\ q \ge 7$. Use strict inequalities only if the question says "unique saddle point". The class notes write $p < 7,\ q > 7$; mention both and you are safe.

3Step 2 · Dominance

Throw away strategies that a sensible player would never use, until the game is small enough for the formula, the graph or the LP.

Dominance reduction

Keep the strategy names (A₃, B₂, …) on every reduced matrix. Never renumber.

StepsMemory hook
1Rows: if row $i$ is ≤ row $k$ in every column, delete row $i$.
Rows: the small one goes. A (greedy) never plays a row that always pays less.
2Columns: if column $j$ is ≥ column $l$ in every row, delete column $j$.
Columns: the big one goes. B (stingy) never plays a column that always costs more.
3Nothing found? Compare a row with the average of two other rows (delete it if ≤ the average) and a column with the average of two others (delete it if ≥ the average).
Two weak friends can beat one. ½ + ½ is the usual mix; any $\lambda, 1-\lambda$ works.
4Repeat steps 1–3 on the smaller matrix. Deleting a row can create a new column dominance, and vice versa.↺ until nothing changes
Rows, columns, rows, columns… until you are stuck.
5What is left? 2×2 → formula; 2×n or m×2 → graph; bigger → LP. Deleted strategies get probability 0.
Label, don't renumber. The final answer is over the original rows and columns.

Cases at this step

Case 3.1

Row dominated by a row

Row ≤ another row in every column; some equalities are fine. In 2025 Q3a: $A_1 = (6, 8, 3)$ ≤ $A_3 = (8, 10, 4)$ ⇒ delete $A_1$.

Case 3.2

Column dominated by a column

Column ≥ another column in every row. In 2025 Q3a: $B_4 = (13, 3, 12, 12)$ ≥ $B_2 = (8, 1, 10, 6)$ ⇒ delete $B_4$.

Case 3.3

Two identical rows (or columns)

Each "dominates" the other. Delete either one; it makes no difference to the value. In the final answer, give the kept one the probability (or split it any way you like between the twins).

Case 3.4

Dominated by an average

No single row beats $A_2$, but $\tfrac12(A_3 + A_4)$ does. In 2025 Q3a: $A_2 = (4, 1, 5)$ and $\tfrac12(A_3 + A_4) = (5.5, 8, 5.5)$, so $A_2$ is ≤ in every place ⇒ delete. For columns, the column must be ≥ the average. Any weights $\lambda, 1 - \lambda$ are allowed; ½–½ is just the usual one to try.

Case 3.5

Chain reaction

After a deletion, compare again: rows that were not comparable before may become comparable. In 2025 Q3a the order is $B_4$, then $A_1$, then $A_2$ (by an average), then $B_2$.

Case 3.6

Reduces to a single row or column

Then the game had a saddle point and you missed it in step 1. The answer is a pure strategy. Go back and show the saddle point check.

Case 3.7

No dominance at all

Say so ("no row is ≤ another row or an average of two rows; no column is ≥ another column or an average of two columns") and move on. Example: the 3×3 game of 2024 Q3c must go to the LP.

Case 3.8

Dominance in a 2×2 game

If one row or column of a 2×2 game dominates the other, there is a saddle point (and conversely). After deleting it you get a 1×2 or 2×1, and the answer is pure.

Trap

Wrong direction

Rows: delete the smaller one. Columns: delete the larger one. Students often delete the larger row ("it's bigger, it dominates" — yes, and that is why you keep it). Also, never compare a row with a column.

Note

Equalities can hide other optimal strategies

Deleting with ≤ (not strict <) never changes the value, but it may throw away a strategy that is also optimal. That is fine for "find the optimal strategies"; only "find all optimal strategies" needs care.

Where the formula comes from (equalising method)

At the optimum, A's mix must make B indifferent between $B_1$ and $B_2$, otherwise B would switch. The class notes write this as "$E(x, 1) = E(x, 2)$". In an exam, either the formula or this equation is fine; the equation is easier to remember under pressure.

You forgot the saddle point check

$\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\1&4\end{bmatrix}$ has a saddle point at $(A_1, B_1)$, with $v = 2$.

The formula gives $D = 6 - 4 = 2$ and $p_1 = \tfrac{4 - 1}{2} = \tfrac32 > 1$: nonsense. A probability outside [0, 1] (or $D = 0$) means there is a saddle point. Always do step 1 first.

Case 4.2

Put negatives in brackets: $D = (-2 + (-6)) - (5 + 7) = -20$, $p_1 = \tfrac{-6 - 7}{-20} = \tfrac{13}{20}$, $q_1 = \tfrac{-6 - 5}{-20} = \tfrac{11}{20}$, $v = \tfrac{12 - 35}{-20} = \tfrac{23}{20}$. A negative D is fine; both numerator and denominator are negative.

The class-notes way: graph + complementary slackness

The class notes set up the graphical method as a small LP, which is the same picture written algebraically. For a 2×n game, A solves

$$\text{Max } v \quad\text{s.t.}\quad E_j(p) = a_{1j}p + a_{2j}(1-p) \ge v\ \ (j = 1, \dots, n),\qquad 0 \le p \le 1 .$$

Each constraint, written as $v + (\dots)p \le (\dots)$, is plotted as the line $v = E_j(p)$; the feasible region lies under all of them (the lower envelope) and the optimum is its highest point. B's problem is the dual, so by complementary slackness:

  • a constraint that holds with equality at the optimum (a line through the point) ⇒ that B strategy may be used ($q_j \ge 0$);
  • a constraint that is strict at the optimum (line above the point) ⇒ $q_j = 0$, so drop that column.

Class-notes example: $\begin{bmatrix}2&2&3&-2\\4&3&2&6\end{bmatrix}$. The constraints are $v + 2p \le 4$, $v + p \le 3$, $v - p \le 2$ and $v + 8p \le 6$. Constraints ③ and ④ meet at the highest point: $v - p = 2$ and $v + 8p = 6$ give $p = \tfrac49$, $v = \tfrac{22}{9}$. ① and ② are strict there, so $q_1 = q_2 = 0$. Then $3y - 2(1 - y) = 2y + 6(1 - y)$ gives $y = \tfrac89$, so B = $(0, 0, \tfrac89, \tfrac19)$ and A = $(\tfrac49, \tfrac59)$. For m×2 it is the same with "Min $v$ s.t. $E_i(q) \le v$" and the upper envelope.

Why the substitution works (2 lines for the theory part)

B wants to minimise $v$ subject to $\sum_j a_{ij}q_j \le v$ for every row $i$, with $\sum q_j = 1$. Divide by $v > 0$ and put $y_j = q_j/v$: the constraints become $\sum_j a_{ij}y_j \le 1$, and $\sum y_j = 1/v$. Minimising $v$ is the same as maximising $\sum y_j$. A's problem, maximise $v$ subject to $\sum_i a_{ij}p_i \ge v$, becomes minimise $\sum x_i$ subject to $\sum_i a_{ij}x_i \ge 1$, which is exactly the dual of B's. Equal optimal values of a primal and its dual = the minimax theorem.

The check (from the class-notes theorem)

With your answer $p^*, q^*, v$:

  1. Multiply A's mix into every column: $E(p^*, B_j) = \sum_i p_i a_{ij}$. Every one must be ≥ v, and = v for the columns B actually uses.
  2. Multiply B's mix into every row: $E(A_i, q^*) = \sum_j a_{ij}q_j$. Every one must be ≤ v, and = v for the rows A actually uses.
  3. Probabilities are in [0, 1] and add to 1; maximin ≤ v ≤ minimax.

Example, 2025 Q3a: A = $(0, 0, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ against the columns gives $5.5, 8, 5.5, 12$ (all ≥ 5.5 ✓). B = $(\tfrac38, 0, \tfrac58, 0)$ against the rows gives $\tfrac{33}{8}, \tfrac{37}{8}, 5.5, 5.5$ (all ≤ 5.5 ✓).

Minimax criterion of optimality

Each player assumes the opponent will make the reply that is worst for them, and picks the strategy whose worst outcome is best. Player A (maximiser) takes the minimum of each row and chooses the row with the largest minimum: maximin $= \max_i \min_j a_{ij}$. Player B (minimiser) takes the maximum of each column and chooses the column with the smallest maximum: minimax $= \min_j \max_i a_{ij}$. Always maximin ≤ $v$ ≤ minimax. If they are equal the game has a saddle point and pure strategies are optimal. Otherwise the criterion is applied to expected payoffs of mixed strategies, and by the minimax theorem $\max_p \min_q E(p, q) = \min_q \max_p E(p, q) = v$.

Endsem 2024 · Q3a Theory: short notesWrite short notes on: (i) saddle point of the game, (ii) mixed strategy vs pure strategy, (iii) two-person zero-sum game, (iv) minimax principle, (v) value of the game.

(i) Saddle point. An entry of the payoff matrix that is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column. It exists when maximin = minimax. The players then use pure strategies (that row and that column), the value equals that entry, and neither player gains by changing alone (an equilibrium point). Example: in $\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\-1&4\end{bmatrix}$, entry (1,1) = 0 is a saddle point.

(ii) Mixed vs pure strategy. A pure strategy is one fixed course of action chosen with certainty every time. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over the pure strategies, $(p_1, \dots, p_m)$ with $p_i \ge 0$ and $\sum p_i = 1$. It is used when there is no saddle point, and the player then optimises the expected payoff. A pure strategy is a mixed strategy with one probability equal to 1. Example: in a coin game, "always Heads" is pure; "Heads with ½, Tails with ½" is mixed.

(iii) Two-person zero-sum game. A game between two players where one's gain equals the other's loss, so the payoffs always add to zero. It is described completely by A's $m \times n$ payoff matrix $(a_{ij})$ (a rectangular game).

(iv) Minimax principle. A chooses the strategy that maximises the minimum gain (maximin $= \max_i \min_j a_{ij}$). B chooses the strategy that minimises the maximum loss (minimax $= \min_j \max_i a_{ij}$). Maximin ≤ value ≤ minimax, with equality iff there is a saddle point. With mixed strategies the two are always equal (minimax theorem).

(v) Value of the game. The expected payoff to A when both players use their optimal strategies, $v = E(p^*, q^*) = \sum_i\sum_j p_i^* a_{ij} q_j^*$. If $v = 0$ the game is fair; if $v > 0$ it favours A.

Definitions

Two-person zero-sum game: a game with two players in which the gain of one equals the loss of the other, so the sum of their payoffs is always zero. Payoff matrix: the $m \times n$ matrix $(a_{ij})$ whose entry $a_{ij}$ is A's gain (B's loss) when A plays its $i$-th strategy and B plays its $j$-th. Value of the game: the expected payoff to A when both players play optimally; if there is a saddle point, it is the saddle entry.

Finding λ (Case 2.8)

"Strictly determinable" means maximin = minimax. λ sits in three cells, so write every row minimum and column maximum as an expression in λ (drop entries that can never be the min or max):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{row minima: } & \min(\lambda, 6, 2) = \min(\lambda, 2), \quad \min(-1, \lambda, -7) = \min(\lambda, -7), \quad \min(-2, 4, \lambda) = \min(\lambda, -2)\\ \text{column maxima: } & \max(\lambda, -1, -2) = \max(\lambda, -1), \quad \max(6, \lambda, 4) = \max(\lambda, 6), \quad \max(2, -7, \lambda) = \max(\lambda, 2) \end{aligned}$$

Since $\min(\lambda, 2) \ge \min(\lambda, -2) \ge \min(\lambda, -7)$, maximin $= \min(\lambda, 2)$. Since $\max(\lambda, -1) \le \max(\lambda, 2) \le \max(\lambda, 6)$, minimax $= \max(\lambda, -1)$. Now solve $\min(\lambda, 2) = \max(\lambda, -1)$ range by range:

Summer 2026 · Q3c Theory with examplesDefine a strategy. What do you mean by pure and mixed strategy? Give examples. Define saddle points. Describe the maximin–minimax principle with an example for a two-person zero-sum game.

Strategy: the rule, decided before the play, by which a player chooses a course of action. Pure strategy: the player always chooses one particular course of action, e.g. a company that always uses "hard bargaining". Mixed strategy: the player chooses among the courses of action with fixed probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_m$ ($\sum p_i = 1$), e.g. a player tossing a coin to decide between Heads and Tails (½, ½). In 2025 Q3a, A's optimal strategy $(0, 0, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ is mixed.

Saddle point: an element of the payoff matrix that is the minimum of its row and the maximum of its column. It occurs when maximin = minimax; the corresponding pure strategies are optimal and the element is the value.

Maximin–minimax principle: A (maximiser) finds the minimum of each row (the least it can get from that strategy) and picks the row with the largest minimum, the maximin. B (minimiser) finds the maximum of each column (the most it can lose) and picks the column with the smallest maximum, the minimax. Maximin ≤ $v$ ≤ minimax; if they are equal, that entry is a saddle point.

Example (class notes): a labour union (A, rows) negotiates with a company (B, columns); the entries are the costs to the company, i.e. the union's gains.
